# What Makes a Sprinter Fast, and Why Ben Johnson's Medal Should Be Returned

The Enhanced Games happened in Las Vegas on the 24th of May 2026, in a stadium built next to a swimming pool on the Strip, in front of 2,500 invited guests who were mostly social media influencers and a press corps that outnumbered the athletes by roughly four to one. The whole thing was organised by an Australian businessman, funded by Peter Thiel and Donald Trump Jr, and was structured around the premise that, if you let elite athletes take whatever pharmaceutical cocktail their doctors will sign off on, the resulting performances would shatter every world record in athletics, swimming and weightlifting. The bonus for breaking a world record was one million US dollars. The bonus for winning your event was 250,000. The athletes had been preparing for months under medical supervision, with full access to anabolic steroids, human growth hormone, peptide cocktails, EPO derivatives, testosterone, and pretty much anything you can imagine that has, at some point in the last forty years, ended an Olympic career.

Fred Kerley ran 9.97 seconds in the 100m. He had publicly predicted that Usain Bolt's 9.58 would, in his own words, get destroyed. His clean personal best is 9.76. Tristan Evelyn won the women's 100m in 11.25 seconds, a time that would not have qualified her out of the heats at most major championships in the past twenty years. Across three sports, dozens of athletes, four hours of competition under a regulatory framework that permitted essentially every substance the World Anti-Doping Code prohibits, one world record fell — Kristian Gkolomeev's 50m freestyle, which was already an unofficially soft record in a swimsuit that the rest of the world's elite swimmers had largely stopped wearing.

This was supposed to be the proof of concept. The thesis of the Enhanced Games was that anti-doping is an artificial limit imposed on human performance and that, if you remove the limit, the performances jump. The Vegas results suggest the opposite. The performances did not jump. They sat well below what the same athletes had produced earlier in their careers running clean against actual competition. And this should make us reconsider, with some urgency, what we think we know about what makes a sprinter fast.

I should declare an interest. I ran 100m as a young man. My personal best was 10.72, which is not elite but is fast enough to know what it feels like to be in the lactic apocalypse of the final 20 metres, and fast enough to have stood at the start line of a race where the man in lane four would go on to run sub-10 and you can feel, in your nervous system before the gun, that you are not in the same conversation. I have a sense of the texture of the thing. And one of the things I know, from having actually run, is that the difference between the people who run 10.72 and the people who run 9.72 is not a question of who is taking what. It is a question of who they are.

Let me make this concrete. Usain Bolt ran 9.58 seconds in Berlin on the 16th of August 2009. The lane draw, set by IAAF seeding which puts the fastest qualifiers in the central lanes, looked like this: Dwain Chambers in lane 1, Marc Burns in lane 2, Daniel Bailey in lane 3, Bolt in lane 4, Tyson Gay in lane 5, Asafa Powell in lane 6, Darvis Patton in lane 7, Richard Thompson in lane 8. The three fastest men in the history of the event to that date were in three adjacent lanes in the middle of the track. Bolt, in lane 4, had Gay in his peripheral vision to the right and Bailey to his left. Bailey ran 9.93 that night. Powell ran 9.84. There was no direction Bolt could look in which someone running close to ten seconds flat was not occupying his visual field. A sprinter's nervous system is not a closed loop. It calibrates against the threat density of the field. When the field is dense, the system drives. When the field is sparse, it does not.

One year earlier in Beijing, the same athlete, with broadly the same training and broadly the same physiology, ran 9.69. He famously decelerated and started celebrating with 15 metres still to run. The Beijing field was strong but it was not the Berlin field. No one was in reach in the closing phase. His system permitted him to disengage. In Berlin, with Gay still proximate at 80m, the system did not permit disengagement. The difference between 9.69 and 9.58 in successive years — 0.11 seconds, which statisticians described as twenty years ahead of the natural progression of the world record — is a difference attributable not to any change in Bolt and not to any change in his pharmacology but to a change in the field he was running against. The field extracted the time.

Now consider Las Vegas. Fred Kerley, in lane four or wherever they put him, with the most pharmacologically enhanced field ever assembled and a one million dollar bonus on the table, ran 9.97. The next-fastest qualifier in his heat was running times in the high tens. There was no Bolt in lane three. There was no Gay in lane five. There was nothing in his visual field that his nervous system needed to drive against. The pharmacology was maximised. The field was reduced to nearly nothing. And the time reflected the absence of the field, not the presence of the substances.

This is what nobody in 1988 understood about Ben Johnson's 9.79. The Seoul final included Carl Lewis at 9.92, Linford Christie at 9.97, Calvin Smith at 9.99, Dennis Mitchell at 10.04. Four men under ten seconds in a single race in an era when sub-10 was rare. The field was, by the standards of its time, comparable to Berlin 2009 by the standards of 2009. The IOC stripped Johnson on the basis that stanozolol metabolites appeared in his urine sample. The popular and institutional verdict that followed treated this as evidence that the 9.79 was a fraudulent time produced by the substance. Nobody asked, at the time or since in any serious institutional way, how much of the 9.79 was attributable to the stanozolol and how much was attributable to Johnson having been one of the fastest people ever to walk the earth running against the densest sprint field his era had produced. The question was not asked because the answer is unwelcome. The honest answer is that nobody knows, that nobody has ever known, and that the science the verdict implicitly invoked has never had the resources to provide such an answer to anyone about any race.

There is a distinction here that the anti-doping framework has spent forty years carefully not making, which is the distinction between detection and attribution. Detection is whether the substance is in the body. Attribution is how much of the performance the substance caused. The first is a question modern analytical chemistry handles with terrifying precision; mass spectrometry can pull picograms of metabolite out of a urine sample. The second is a question for which there is no procedure. No anti-doping laboratory has ever told the world how many hundredths of a second any particular substance contributed to any particular performance by any particular athlete. The framework operates as if detection were sufficient, and the public understanding of doping has been built on the silent assumption that the substance must have done the work because the substance was found. If the substance did the work, you should be able to see the substance in the performance without needing to look in the body. The fact that you cannot, that the entire detection apparatus exists precisely because you cannot, is itself the evidence. The relationship between substance and performance is too noisy to read backwards. And if it is too noisy to read backwards, the moral grading we have imposed on it cannot be sustained.

The Enhanced Games inadvertently performed the experiment. They took elite sprinters, gave them the full pharmacological toolkit, and put them in a competitive context stripped of dense elite competition. If the doping was the variable that mattered most, the times should have been historic. They were not. The Kerley time would have finished last in the Paris 2024 Olympic final. The Evelyn time would not have made finals at any World Championships in the past quarter century. The pharmacology, isolated from the field, did not produce elite performance. The field, on the available evidence across both Berlin and Beijing and Vegas, does far more causal work than the substances do.

There is a longer argument here that I have made elsewhere, about the way elite sprint performance emerges from the phase-coherent coupling of physiological systems across many scales, about how 78 per cent of the variance in elite 400m performance is accounted for by anthropometric and physiological parameters before pharmacology enters the model at all, about how lane position alone — through the geometry of curve running — accounts for 0.21 seconds of variation in elite 400m times. I will spare you the equations because the conclusion does not require them. The conclusion is that sprint performance is the convergence of many things, of which the substance in the urine sample is one, and not the largest. The 1988 verdict against Ben Johnson selected the smallest of the relevant variables and assigned it categorical moral weight, while treating the larger variables as inert backdrop. This is not how athletic causation works. It is not how it has ever worked. The verdict was a cultural performance, dressed in the institutional clothing of scientific certainty, and the clothing has now been pulled away.

Six of the eight finalists in the Seoul 1988 100m were subsequently implicated in the use, supply, or facilitation of performance-enhancing substances. The man who received Johnson's gold medal had failed three doping tests at the US Olympic Trials two months earlier — three samples, three positives for stimulants — and the United States Olympic Committee had buried the results and sent him to Seoul anyway. Linford Christie, promoted to silver, tested positive at those same Seoul Games for pseudoephedrine and was cleared on the explanation that he had drunk too much ginseng tea, and was later banned for two years in 1999 for nandrolone. Dennis Mitchell, fifth in Seoul, was later sanctioned after a testosterone failure for which his defence — that elevated levels had resulted from consensual sexual activity and several bottles of beer the previous evening — was initially accepted by USA Track and Field and only later rejected. Desai Williams, sixth, admitted after the Dubin Inquiry that he had been on the same programme as Johnson. The race was a contaminated field across the board. Johnson was the one caught. He was punished as if he had been the one who cheated, when he was, more accurately, the one who had been detected. There is a difference, and the difference matters, and the institutional treatment of the difference for the past thirty-eight years has been a slow accumulation of injustice that the Enhanced Games have now made unavoidable.

Restore the medal. Not because Johnson was clean, because he was not, and his coach Charlie Francis spent the rest of his career being open about that. Restore the medal because the moral framework under which he was uniquely and permanently condemned has not survived contact with the present. Restore it because the verdict required the public to believe that stanozolol was a kind of pharmacological magic that converted ordinary athletes into superhumans, and the Enhanced Games have now shown that no such magic exists. Restore it because the field he ran in was contaminated and only he paid. Restore it because the substance in his urine sample was one variable among many and not the largest, and the science the verdict invoked has never had the capacity to tell us how much of the 9.79 the substance produced. Restore it because the man who got his gold had three positive tests two months before the race and the IOC knew. Restore it because, on the long historical scale, the moral grading imposed on Ben Johnson in September 1988 was a cultural reflex, not a scientific finding, and a cultural reflex can be reconsidered when the culture has moved on. The culture has moved on. The Enhanced Games are publicly traded on the Nasdaq. The substance Johnson was destroyed for using is now sold by direct-to-consumer pharmacies under medical supervision in the same country whose Olympic Committee buried Lewis's positive tests. The world that condemned him no longer exists. The condemnation should not outlive the world that produced it.

What does it mean to be fast? It means to be the convergence of an athlete, a moment, a field, a track, a set of conditions, a long pipeline of development that started before the athlete was born. It means to be the place where many causes meet. It does not mean to be the place where one cause was detected. Johnson's 9.79, like Bolt's 9.58, like every elite sprint performance ever produced, was the meeting point of many causes. The substance in the urine sample was one of them. It was not the largest, and it was not, by any honest reading of the science the verdict invoked, separable from the others in a way that would justify the categorical moral grade that has stood for thirty-eight years.

Give him his medal back.
